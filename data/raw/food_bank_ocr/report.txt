THE TRUSSELL TRUST FOODBANK NETWORK IN 2012:
An introduction

The Trussell Trust’s UK foodbank network partners with communities nationwide to launch foodbanks that
provide three days of emergency food to men, women and children in crisis.

The economic downturn has seen the need for foodbanks soar right across the UK. New foodbanks are

opening at the rate of three a week and the number of people fed by foodbanks has doubled to almost
130,000 in the last year alone.

The simple, practical gift of food and support at the point of crisis changes lives.
The use of a highly effective and pioneering social franchise model has enabled The Trussell Trust to
respond incredibly quickly, sensitively and effectively to the growing need for emergency food in the

UK by helping communities to start foodbanks. In April 2012 The Trussell Trust launched our 200th UK

foodbank a year ahead of our target. Our vision is to have a foodbank in every town, creating a nation
where no-one has to go hungry.

THE BEGINNINGS OF SOMETHING BIG...
Our history

‘Lcan’t feed my children tonight and | live around the corner from you in
Salisbury’

-a local mum to Trussell Trust founder, Paddy Henderson, 1999

A phone call from a distressed Salisbury mum in 1999 triggered the start of a nationwide network
of foodbanks that has since given emergency food to over 374,000 people in crisis across the UK.

At the time of the phonecall, Trussell Trust founders Paddy and Carol Henderson were working in

Bulgaria with abandoned children but this call prompted them to research the problem of ‘hidden
hunger’ on their own doorstep in Salisbury. They were shocked by what they found. Local indices

of deprivation combined with reports from social services confirmed that thousands of people on

low incomes hit crisis and struggle to feed themselves and their families as a result.

Paddy and Carol Henderson’s response to the problem of short-term hunger was innovative and
practical — they set up a ‘foodbank’, in their garden shed. The public ‘deposited’ non-perishable
food and ‘withdrawals’ were made by those in need, when they produced a properly authorised
voucher, To be sure that they were reaching those in genuine need, they asked frontline care
professionals (social workers, doctors, teachers etc) to act as their ears and eyes in the community,
and to issue foodbank vouchers.

In its first year, Salisbury foodbank fed 600 people and each year the numbers fed grew. Salisbury
was not the only community with ‘hidden hunger’; this was an idea which had the potential to
help thousands of people around the country. How could others be helped to avoid the mistakes
they had made and be empowered to set up their own foadbank quickly and efficiently? In 2004,
we developed one of the UK’s first social franchises: the foodbank network.

Since 2004 The Trussell Trust has launched over 250 foodbanks nationwide.

HOW DOES A FOODBANK WORK?

Foodbanks are run by the community for the community: non-perishable food is donated by local people
- schools, businesses, churches and individuals. Volunteers of all backgrounds help run foodbanks by
packing, sorting and distributing the food. Every client is referred to the foodbank by a frontline care
professional such as a doctor or social worker. Foodbanks are an emergency food service: to prevent
dependency on foodbanks our clients are entitled to up to three consecutive foodbank vouchers. To help
clients break out of poverty, we signpost clients to organisations able to resolve the underlying problem.

Food parcels have been designed by dieticians to provide recipients with nutritionally balanced food for
three days. Rural foodbanks often operate delivery services to reach those unable to get to a foodbank.

CASE STUDIES

T hadn't eaten for days to feed yay children’

William, 27, gave up his job at the Odeon to start a career in the Army to provide a secure future for his
family. Will’s income decreased as food prices rose. Whilst he was away on basic training his wife Annie,
25, started going without food to feed their two young daughters. The family had not been provided with
army accommodation and were living in a one bedroom flat with their two daughters aged four and 18
months. Determined not to get into debt, Annie was forced to go without food to pay the bills and feed
her children. The increasing stress and hunger overwhelmed Annie when dropping off her daughter at
nursery one day. She broke down in tears and they referred her to the foodbank. She said ‘now that | have
food | fee! able to face the rest of my difficutties’ The family are now in military accommodation and are
back on their feet.

taneml expenses meant we couldn't afford food’ says 13-year-old Sophie

When 13-year-old Sophie's father became terminally ill, her mum gave up work to look after him. When
he passed away, the situation got dramatically worse as the grieving family found themselves crippled by
funeral costs and unable to afford food.

Chesham Foodbank gave the family enough emergency food to help them through their crisis. Sophie said
‘it was such a massive help and the people there were really kind” She said that the food parcel helped
them to get back on their feet and that without it ‘it would have been impossible to cope’

Receiving a food parcel helped to reduce the family’s anxiety and meant that they could concentrate on
supporting each other, Sophie said that the foodbank ‘made it a little bit easier to feel better’.

Police take desperate shoplifter to foodbank

Rather than arresting a young man when he was caught shoplifting in Islington, police officers took him

to the foodbank recently. Adam had not eaten for four days and had finally resorted to stealing bread and
eggs froma local shop. After experiencing 23 days benefit delay Adam had tried begging for food and was
desperate. Arrested by plain clothes officers, he was distraught at having been forced to turn to crime to
feed himelf. Fortunately, the police officers were so moved by his plight that they took him to {slington
Foodbank. Adam cried with gratitude when he saw the bags of food and the police were so impressed by the
foodbank service that they asked to become official foodbank voucher holders.

‘| cried with joy when our foodbank Christmas hamper was delivered, says Mandi, a mother of two, ‘it was

such a relief: we were going to eat beans on toast for Christmas dinner but instead we could have a festive

Despite being a working family, reduced hours and salary had left the family unable to make ends meet as

first time in weeks. We'd been telling the children we'd eat when they’d gone to bed, but the reality was
we were going without. Salisbury foodbank made our Christmas, it was the best we've ever had.

Escaping domestic abuse meant that T ended up at a toodbank'

Alison, 59, sobbed as she told her story at a foodbank centre recently. Having worked all her life she

was so embarrassed to have to turn to a charity to be able to eat. The problem came when she finally
escaped from a long and abusive marriage. Sharing a workplace with her husband, she had to leave her
employment. After sending hundreds of job applications without success, Alison found herself without
anything. Her friends couldn’t help anymore and she was trying to support her son too. In despair, she
turned to the foodbank for help. After years of abuse she was so moved by the care she received from the

foodbank team that she said her hope in people had been restored and ‘I was made to feel | belonged to
the-human race,

21 year-old college student Gemma had not eaten properly for weeks and could not afford gas to heat
her flat when she came to the foodbank in January. After leaving state care she was determined to make
something of her life and was funding herself through college. Working two evening jobs to make ends
meet, she was made redundant from both within a month. As a 21-year-old in full time education with

no children she did not qualify for benefits, She sold everything she had and, with nowhere else to turn,
she asked her local councillor for help; they referred her to the foodbank. When she arrived she was ill,
dehydrated and in the first stages of malnutrition. For Gemma, the foodbank was a life saver. She says she
has no idea what she would have done without it. She was so impressed by the support she received that
she began volunteering at the foodbank.

Despite suffering from cerebral palsy, Charlie, 31, leads the volunteers at Salisbury foodbank centre
every Tuesday and is the longest standing foodbank volunteer in the country. Her disability meant that
her application to volunteer was turned down at many other charities but her kind, warm manner has
been invaluable at the foodbank. Charlie has now been volunteering for The Trussell Trust for 11 years
and recently carried the Olympic torch in recognition of her contribution to the local community. Charlie
says ‘It is a great feeling to go home after a day at the foodbank centre knowing that someone won't

go hungry that night. If | didn’t come to The Trussell Trust, I'd be sitting at home on my own. Coming
here gives me a purpose, The Trussell Trust’s supported volunteer programme recognises the valuable
contribution that people of all backgrounds and abilities can make in their local communities.

feeding our chidren during the school holidays tipped us over the edge’

For agricultural engineer Ed and his wife Lizzie, the recession has been all too real. A pretty village in the
Cotswolds is not where you expect to find poverty but rural poverty can bite hardest. Ed, Lizzie and their
children are victims of the economic downturn. After time off due to a back injury, Ed struggled to find
enough work to pay the bills. When the school holidays began and the children did not have access to
free school meals, Ed and Lizzie started going without food to make sure that the children were fed.

North Cotswold Foodbank manager James Milton says: ‘The combination of higher living costs and lower
earnings puts immense pressure on working people in rural communities. The majority of our clients are
working families. The national minimum wage might be sufficient for survival in towns, where amenities
are within walking distance. In rural communities, it cannot stretch to the additional costs of essential
travel, higher prices, and higher domestic energy costs. To achieve an acceptable standard of living, an
urban parent with one child needs to earn £6.37 per hour, for a parent in a village this figure jumps to
£10.13. There’s an old saying in the countryside: “You can’t eat the view”, it’s more true today than ever’

Changes to mi ESA left me with no money for food’

Edna, almost 60, was referred to the foodbank by a cancer charity after her Employment Support
Allowance was replaced by Job Seeker’s Allowance. Despite having undergone radical surgery to remove
cancer last year and suffering from arthritis and depression following the recent death of her brother,
Edna was told she was fit for work. She has been applying for jobs with little success and feels that her age
has reduced her employability. She is also concerned that she would not be able to physically cope with

a job and is appealing the decision but this is expected to take weeks. Edna lives alone and has sold her
TV, a main source of company for her, to help put food on the table and has been eating plain pasta or
couscous as it’s all she can afford. She contacted the foodbank with just £8 left to last her five days.

MEETING THE NEED:

How you can become part of the solution to hidden
hunger in the UK

communities and churches to open foodbanks nationwide. We urgently need to generate much-needed funds to
underpin this exceptional growth.

By supporting The Trussell Trust’s foodbank network, you will help to prevent people from going hungry in their own
homes. You will help to stop parents from skipping meals to feed their children, or people having to decide between
eating and paying the rent.

Your support will encourage local communities to be part of the solution to local food poverty.

The foodbank network is part of The Trussell Trust

The Trussell Trust empowers local communities to combat poverty and marginalisation in the UK and
Bulgaria. We develop innovative, sustainable projects such as foodbanks, community shops and supported
volunteering programmes in the UK as well as opening Houses of Opportunity for orphanage leavers in
Bulgaria. We work with the people that society forgets, providing practical help and enabling each person
to realise that they are valued. Our vision is to build communities where people of all backgrounds are
included and have the opportunity to live in dignity with hope for the future.
